Historical manhwa mix real customs with invented ones. Each trope below is compared with real history and given a verdict.

The cold northern Grand Duke

A bit of both

In the manhwa

The Grand Duke rules a frozen northern territory, outranks every other noble, and is feared even by the emperor.

In history

Grand dukes were real. Some ruled their own small states, such as Luxembourg, and in Russia the title went to the emperor's sons and grandsons. The lonely monster of the north is invented.

The contract marriage

Mostly fiction

In the manhwa

The heroine signs a secret contract to marry the duke for one year, then divorce him and leave with a payment.

In history

Marriages really did come with contracts, but they covered money and land, not an end date. Divorce in England was almost impossible before 1857 and needed an Act of Parliament.

The debutante ball

Fact

In the manhwa

The heroine's debut ball is her first appearance in society, and everyone is watching her dress and her first dance.

In history

Very close to history. Young women were formally presented at court, wore white, and were expected to start looking for a husband after their debut.

The daughter who inherits the dukedom

Mostly fiction

In the manhwa

The heroine becomes the next duchess in her own right and runs the family territory herself.

In history

Most noble titles passed only to sons or male relatives. A few older titles could pass to daughters, but a woman holding a dukedom in her own right was very rare.

One scandal ruins a lady

Fact

In the manhwa

A rumor that the heroine was alone with a man spreads overnight, and suddenly she must marry him or lose her place in society.

In history

This is real. A young lady's reputation was closely guarded, which is why chaperones existed. Being caught alone with a man could force an engagement.

The loyal knight bodyguard

A bit of both

In the manhwa

A sword-carrying knight swears loyalty to the heroine and follows her everywhere as her personal guard.

In history

Medieval knights really were warriors who served a lord. By the time of gowns, balls, and tea parties, though, knighthood was mostly an honor given by the king, not a job guarding a lady.
